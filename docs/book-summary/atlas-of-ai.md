**Introduction**
The introduction establishes the book's central argument: **artificial intelligence is neither artificial nor intelligent**. Instead of being an abstract, disembodied entity, AI is highly material, built from natural resources, human labor, infrastructures, and massive datasets. Crawford introduces the metaphor of an "atlas" to map out the hidden topographies of computation and to demonstrate how **AI functions as an extractive industry and a registry of power** designed to serve dominant interests. 

**Chapter 1: Earth**
This chapter focuses on the immense material and environmental costs required to power planetary computation. Crawford traces the physical origins of AI to **the extraction of minerals like lithium and rare earth elements** from mines in places like Nevada, Mongolia, and the Congo—processes that often involve ecological devastation and human suffering. The chapter dismantles the myth of a "clean" tech industry by highlighting the **massive energy consumption, carbon footprints, and water usage of data centers** and global logistical networks.

**Chapter 2: Labor**
Crawford examines the hidden human labor that props up the illusion of autonomous machine intelligence. **AI relies on a vast, exploited workforce**, from warehouse workers enduring strict algorithmic surveillance and punishing quotas (like at Amazon) to digital "crowdworkers" (such as Amazon Mechanical Turks) who are paid pennies to label data, moderate content, and correct algorithmic errors. The chapter connects contemporary AI labor practices to the history of Taylorism and the micromanagement of workers' time and bodies.

**Chapter 3: Data**
This section explores the history and ideology of mass data extraction. Crawford critiques the tech industry's "collect-it-all" mentality, where **data is viewed as a free, natural resource to be harvested without consent or context**. Using examples like the NIST mugshot database and ImageNet, she shows how personal photos, internet texts, and biometric traces are stripped of their human context and transformed into mere infrastructure for training machine learning algorithms. 

**Chapter 4: Classification**
Crawford investigates the politics and harms embedded in how AI categorizes the world. Drawing parallels to 19th-century craniometry (the pseudoscience of measuring skulls to determine intelligence and character), she argues that **AI classification systems enforce harmful hierarchies and essentialize race and gender**. Rather than being a simple technical bug, she argues that bias is a foundational feature of classification, reducing human complexity to rigid, computational binaries.

**Chapter 5: Affect**
This chapter critiques the booming and highly profitable industry of **automated emotion recognition**. Crawford traces the origins of this field to psychologist Paul Ekman's controversial theories that humans possess a universal set of readable facial expressions. Despite a lack of scientific consensus that interior feelings can be accurately determined from external facial movements, these flawed AI systems are increasingly being deployed in high-stakes environments like hiring, education, and policing.

**Chapter 6: State**
Crawford explores the deep and ongoing collaboration between the AI industry and state power, particularly military and intelligence agencies. Looking at the Snowden archive and the Pentagon's Project Maven, she details how **militarized surveillance tools and targeting logics have migrated into domestic life**. Through private contractors like Palantir, systems designed for warfare are now used for predictive policing, immigration enforcement, and welfare adjudication.

**Conclusion: Power**
The conclusion reinforces the idea that **AI systems amplify structural inequalities and centralize control for corporations and the state**. Crawford warns against "enchanted determinism"—the belief that AI is magical, objective, and inevitable—which obscures the actual power dynamics at play. She calls for a "politics of refusal" and urges the public to connect movements for labor rights, climate justice, and data protection to challenge the extractive logics of planetary computation.

**Coda: Space**
In the final section, Crawford analyzes the "billionaire space race" led by tech elites like Jeff Bezos and Elon Musk. She frames their ambitions for space colonization and asteroid mining as **the ultimate imperial extension of the tech industry's extractive mindset**, driven by a desire for endless economic growth and an escape from the ecological limits and regulations of Earth.
