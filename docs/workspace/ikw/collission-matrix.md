
When you have students who are not interested in coding, asking them to "think of an app idea" will result in blank stares, anxiety, or highly generic answers like "an app that tracks homework." They freeze because they don't know what is technically possible, and they don't see how their own majors connect to a piece of software.

To break the ice instantly, you need to use a **"Collision Matrix"**—a structured brainstorming game that forces their different majors together to spark an idea automatically.

Here is how you kick off Day 1 and guide them from zero ideas to a validated project in the first 20 minutes.

---

## Step 1: The "Major Collision" Idea Generator (First 10 Mins)

Draw a simple matrix on the board. On one axis, put **Aviation Tech**. On the other axis, put the **other majors** represented in the room (Business, Design, Humanities, etc.).


Instead of building a boring corporate website, give them a massive corporate brand context to frame it like a real-world enterprise project. For instance, imagine a tech giant like Samsung Electronics America Inc.—which has its enterprise offices right in the Seattle metro area over in Bellevue—is hiring their student team to design a brand new "Aviation Smart Solutions" digital product line.

Give them 3 plug-and-play **Idea Templates** that naturally bridge their skills:

### 🎮 Option A: The Game Route (High Engagement)

* **The Blueprint:** A 2D simulation game where a pilot has to navigate a crisis, but a business manager has to control the costs.
* **The Idea Spark:** **"Crosswind Tycoon."** Aviation students input the real physics (how wind speed impacts a Boeing 737 landing). Business/Design students build the "Airport Budget" interface. If the pilot aborts too many landings due to wind, the airport loses money on fuel.
* **Why it works:** It feels like a real game, but it requires aviation knowledge and business logic to design the scoring system.

### 📊 Option B: The Operational Dashboard (High Practicality)

* **The Blueprint:** A live control panel for an airport manager or airline dispatcher.
* **The Idea Spark:** **"The Turnaround Bottleneck Simulator."** When a plane lands, a countdown timer starts. Ground crews must load luggage, refuel, and board passengers. Students create a dashboard with sliders for "Number of Baggage Handlers" and "Weather Delays" to simulate how much money an airline loses for every minute a plane sits at the gate.
* **Why it works:** Aviation students understand the operational pain points; Business students understand the cost tracking.

### 📱 Option C: The Smart Hardware Interface (The "Samsung" Angle)

* **The Blueprint:** Designing the user interface for a flight deck tablet or an airport kiosk.
* **The Idea Spark:** **"Pilot Fatigue & Fuel Telemetry."** An app that takes a pilot's scheduled flight hours (Aviation/Human Factors) and calculates their alertness alongside the plane's current fuel burn rates.
* **Why it works:** Perfect for mixed genders and majors because it blends human psychology, visual design, and strict aviation rules.

---

## Step 2: How to Facilitate the Room (The First 15 Minutes of Group Work)

Once you split them into their mixed groups, do not let them sit quietly. Walk the room and use these exact prompts to get them talking:

1. **Find the Expert:** Walk up to a group and say to the Aviation student: *"Tell your team the single most stressful or annoying thing you have to calculate or deal with in the hangar or the cockpit."* (e.g., "Weight and balance distribution before takeoff").
2. **Hand Off to the Non-Coder:** Turn to the Business or Design student in that group and say: *"Great. Your aviation teammate just gave you the problem. If you were building a dashboard to sell to an airline manager to fix that, what numbers would that manager want to see flashing on the screen?"* (e.g., "Total cost of wasted fuel" or "Safety hazard warning level").
3. **Appoint the "Vibe Director":** Assign the student who is the least interested in coding to be the Lead Prompt Engineer. Tell them: *"Your job is to take this conversation, put it into the Day 1 Worksheet template, and dictate the orders to the AI. You are the boss of the software."*

---

## Step 3: Driving the Iteration Loop (So they don't get bored)

Once they get their first basic script running via the AI, they might say, *"Okay, we have a slider that changes numbers. Now what?"*

Teach them how to **Iterate through Drama**. Tell them to prompt the AI to add unexpected constraints to their simulation:

* *"Add a 'Storm Mode' button that multiplies all the wind risk numbers by 3 and turns the dashboard background red."*
* *"Add a 'Budget Cut' slider that reduces the number of available ground crew and calculates how many flights get delayed as a result."*

By gamifying the parameters and treating the AI as a flexible canvas, the students will start competing to see whose simulation or mini-game is the most dynamic, entirely bypassing the boredom of traditional syntax learning.

## What is Collision Matrix

A **Collision Matrix** is a lightweight brainstorming tool that forces two completely unrelated categories to collide, instantly generating unexpected ideas.

When you ask a mixed group of students to "think of an app," they freeze because the blank canvas is intimidating. A collision matrix solves this by turning brainstorming into a structured game of **connect-the-dots**.

On the whiteboard, you cross-reference **Domain Problems** (from the aviation students) with **Major Disciplines** (from the business, design, or humanities students). Where the rows and columns meet, an app idea is born.

Here is what a quick classroom matrix looks like on the board:

|  | **Design / Creative Majors** *(Focus: Visuals, UI, Gamification)* | **Business / Management Majors** *(Focus: Cost, Logistics, Efficiency)* | **Humanities / Psychology** *(Focus: Human factors, Stress, Safety)* |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Aviation Tech: Crosswind Landings** | **"Crosswind Arcade"**<br>

<br>A 2D visual landing simulator where you drag a slider to control wind velocity. | **"Diverted Flight Calculator"**<br>

<br>Simulates the exact dollar cost an airline loses when a plane has to divert due to wind. | **"Pilot Stress Tracker"**<br>

<br>An interface that simulates a pilot's heart rate climbing as wind speeds increase. |
| **Aviation Tech: Weight & Balance** | **"Cargo Tetris"**<br>

<br>A visual game where stacking heavy luggage too far back causes the plane graphic to tilt dangerously. | **"Fuel Burn Estimator"**<br>

<br>A dashboard showing how adding 500 lbs of extra cargo drains fuel costs over a 3-hour flight. | **"Baggage Crew Burnout"**<br>

<br>A slider-based tool showing how heavy cargo distributions delay gate turns and fatigue ground crews. |

---

### How to use it in the classroom (The 5-Minute Setup)

1. **Draw the blank grid:** Put the Aviation topics on the left side and the other majors across the top.
2. **Crowdsource the rows:** Ask an aviation student, *"What is something simple you have to calculate or worry about?"* Write it on the left (e.g., *Fuel planning* or *Pre-flight inspection checklists*).
3. **Run the "Collision":** Point to a square and challenge the team. *"Group 3, you have a business major and a design major. Look at 'Fuel Planning'. You are now building an app for Samsung's aviation division. Cross fuel planning with business logistics. What does that app track?"*
4. **Select and Lock:** Within five minutes, every group will have pointed to a specific box on the board and said, *"We want to build that one."*

This completely eliminates "blank page syndrome." It gives the aviation students instant authority as the subject-matter experts, and gives the non-coders a clear, non-technical lens through which to design the product.

The term **"Collision Matrix"** is used across a few different fields, ranging from engineering to corporate strategy, but what we are doing here—using a matrix to force two different categories together to spark a new concept—is fundamentally rooted in **Design Thinking, Cross-Disciplinary Innovation, and Combinatorial Creativity**.

In academic literature and product design research, this tool and its underlying methodology are frequently studied under the following concepts:

---

## 1. Morphological Analysis & Ideation Matrices

In design and engineering research, your whiteboard exercise is structurally known as **General Morphological Analysis (GMA)** or a **Morphological Matrix**. Developed by astrophysicist Fritz Zwicky, it is a method for exploring all possible relationships in a multi-dimensional, non-quantifiable problem space.

* **The Research:** Studies on engineering education show that when students use structural matrices rather than free-form brainstorming, they generate a significantly higher *novelty* and *variety* of concepts.
* **Key Concept:** By fixing variables on an $X$ and $Y$ axis, you bypass human cognitive bias (the tendency to only think of ideas we are already comfortable with) and force the brain to fill in a "logical gap."

## 2. Combinatorial Creativity & Interdisciplinary Education

In educational psychology, the act of forcing an aviation tech major to collide with a business major to create a software feature is called **Combinatorial Creativity** or **Conceptual Blending**.

* **The Research:** Creative genius isn't usually about inventing something entirely from scratch; it’s about taking two existing concepts that have never met and "colliding" them (e.g., *Aviation Constraints + Retro Arcade Mechanics = Crosswind Tycoon*).
* **Why it works for non-coders:** Research into multi-disciplinary STEM classrooms shows that when you anchor a technical task in a non-technical student’s domain expertise (like business or psychology), their "stereotype threat" or fear of the technology drops dramatically. They realize they don't need to know how to code to bring immense value to the architecture of the system.

## 3. Product Management & Corporate Frameworks

In the enterprise product space (very relevant if you are framing this as a project for a company like Samsung), this exact framework is often adapted as an **Innovation Matrix** or a **Feature Matrix**.

* **The Corporate Application:** Large tech companies frequently use matrices to align engineering capability with market needs. Rows might represent core technology stacks (like *Physical AI / Digital Twins*), while columns represent market verticals (like *Commercial Aviation, Healthcare, Logistics*). The intersections dictate the product roadmap.

### 🎨 How Students Use It: "Pick Your Own Adventure"

Before they write their prompt for the AI, a student team simply circles one item per row. By connecting the circles, they build a highly specific project architecture.

Here are two completely different combinations generated from the exact same matrix:

* **Combination Alpha (The Game Route):** `1-4` ➔ `2-3` ➔ `3-1` ➔ `4-3`
* *The Result:* A **Flight School Student** learning about **Unexpected Crosswinds** using a **2D Simulation / Game** that incorporates a **Psychology angle** tracking pilot stress levels as the wind increases.


* **Combination Beta (The Corporate Dashboard Route):** `1-2` ➔ `2-1` ➔ `3-2` ➔ `4-1`
* *The Result:* An **Airport Gate Manager** optimizing **Weight & Balance Limits** using a **Real-time Dashboard** that incorporates a **Business angle** calculating the exact operational dollar costs of delaying the flight.

This completely gamifies the launch phase and guarantees that no two student teams in the room will build the exact same app.

Once the board is messy and full of their actual words, you can pull those exact items straight into the Matrix columns. Here is how you can execute this seamlessly on Day 1:

---

## 🧠 The 10-Minute "Brain Dump" Board Protocol

Walk up to the board, draw three massive, colorful circles, and challenge the room. Tell them: *"Before we look at software, we need raw material. Shouting out answers is mandatory."*

### 1. Circle 1: The Domain Pain Points (Aviation Majors Lead)

* **The Prompt:** *"Aviation students, what drives you absolutely crazy? What are you forced to calculate by hand? What goes wrong at the airport or in flight school?"*
* **What goes on the board:** *Crosswinds, weight limits, fuel calculations, maintenance checklists, scheduling delays, communicating with ground crews.*

### 2. Circle 2: The Opportunities & Brands (Everyone Connects)

* **The Prompt:** *"If we are building this product line under a massive enterprise tech umbrella like Samsung, what hardware or environments are we playing with? Where does this app live?"*
* **What goes on the board:** *Smartwatches for pilots, flight deck tablets, massive digital airport gate displays, mobile apps, simulation cockpits.*

### 3. Circle 3: The Human & Business Angles (Mixed Majors Lead)

* **The Prompt:** *"Everyone else—Business, Design, Humanities—when these aviation problems happen, what breaks down? What do people care about?"*
* **What goes on the board:** *Losing money, pilot fatigue/stress, angry passengers, bad visual design, visual alarms, safety risk levels.*

---

## 🔄 The Pivot: Moving from Mind Map to Morphological Matrix

Once the board is covered in their ideas, the transition to the matrix is where the magic happens. You show them how to turn chaos into architecture.

1. **Draw the blank matrix grid** right next to the mind map.
2. **Grab their words:** Physically point to the mind map, circle a few items, and drop them directly into the matrix rows.
* *"Look at what you all wrote. We have 'Crosswinds' and 'Weight limits' under pain points. Those are our **Aviation Headaches** (Row 2). We have 'Smartwatches' and 'Gate displays'. Those are our **User Interfaces**."*


3. **The Reveal:** *"Now, look at how easy it is to design a company product."* Draw a single line connecting a few of their circled words across the grid. *"Boom. Team 1, you are building a pilot stress-tracker app for a Samsung smartwatch that triggers a visual alarm during high-crosswind landings. Open your laptops, your blueprint is done."*

### 💡 Why this order works beautifully

By doing the mind map first, the students see **their own ideas** populate the formal matrix. It gives them immediate ownership over the curriculum, proves that their individual majors are highly relevant to the project, and gives them an abundant sandbox of raw materials to play with before they ever type a prompt into an LLM.
