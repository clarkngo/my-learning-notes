# Reframe the Narrative: "The Floater" = "The Firefighter"
Management often undervalues stability until it is gone. If she was moving between projects, fixing bugs, and working on features that were scrapped, she was likely absorbing the instability of the team so others could focus.

She needs to position herself not as a "floater," but as:

**The Utility Player:** Someone capable of high-context switching (a difficult cognitive skill).

**The Risk Mitigator:** Prototyping features that were scrapped is actually a success. It is "failing fast." She saved the company money by proving an idea wasn't viable before they invested more time into it.

**The Debt Collector:** Fixing "unimportant" bugs is actually paying down technical debt, which prevents the product from collapsing later.

Here are a few ways to say those sentences casually, depending on how close she is with her manager.

### Option 1: Chill & Collaborative (Best for a good relationship)

**The "Future" Ask:**
> "I feel like I’ve shown I can roll with the punches and jump into whatever code needs attention. For next year, though, I’d really love to take that experience and actually own a project from start to finish. I’m ready to stop just supporting the roadmap and start driving a piece of it myself."

**The "Value" Statement:**
> "I think my main strength this year was just being flexible. I know I didn't have one massive feature launch, but I plugged a lot of holes for the team. I picked up the stuff that was slipping through the cracks and did the legwork on those prototypes so we could make the right calls. I was basically the glue keeping the backlog healthy."

### Option 2: Direct & Confident (Best if she needs to be firm)

**The "Future" Ask:**
> "I’ve proven I can handle the chaos and work in any part of the stack. Next year, I want to use that context to own a specific domain end-to-end. I want to shift from just filling in gaps to actually leading a feature."

**The "Value" Statement:**
> "This year was really about adaptability for me. I didn't own one specific vertical, but I handled the critical stuff that no one else had bandwidth for. Whether it was clearing out the backlog or vetting those experimental features, I focused on doing the 'glue work' that kept the team moving."

### Option 3: Short & Punchy (Best for a quick check-in)

**The "Future" Ask:**
> "I’ve shown I can jump into any fire. Next year, I want to channel that into owning a specific product area. I’m ready to be in the driver’s seat on a feature rather than just riding shotgun."

**The "Value" Statement:**
> "I was the utility player this year. I didn't ship one giant thing, but I kept the lights on by tackling the tickets nobody else could get to and vetting features before we over-committed. I kept the process unblocked."

# The Cool Table

This is the most critical pivot she needs to make. In engineering, **you are rarely invited to the cool table; you have to pull up a chair.**

Here is a step-by-step game plan she can use to ensure she is locked into the core group for the next big project.

### 1. The "Anchor" Strategy (Technical Entry)
The easiest way to get cut from a group is to be a "generalist" on the project. If she is just "helping out," she is optional. She needs to own a piece of the project that **forces** the group to include her.

* **The Move:** Volunteer for a specific, necessary component early.
* **Examples:**
    * *"I’ll handle the API schema design."* (Now everyone *has* to talk to her to agree on data formats).
    * *"I’ll own the logging and telemetry setup."* (She now needs to be in every meeting to know what to log).
    * *"I’ll write the integration tests."* (She has to understand the code deeply to test it).
* **Why it works:** She becomes a dependency. They *can't* have the meeting without her because they need her component.

### 2. Negotiate the "Floater Tax" *Before* the Project Starts
She failed last time because she was likely drowning in "keep the lights on" (KTLO) work while the others were building.

* **The Conversation with her Manager:**
    > "For this next project to succeed, I need to be 80% focused on it. That means I can't be the primary person answering support tickets or fixing random bugs during this sprint. **Who is going to cover the floater tasks while I focus on this?**"
* If she doesn't get this agreement, she will inevitably fall behind again.

### 3. Aggressive Calendar Management
In fast-moving subgroups, meetings often happen spontaneously or "shadow" invites are created.

* **The "Shadow" Tactic:** She needs to watch the calendars of the project leads. If she sees a recurring meeting called "Sync," "Arch Review," or "Quick Huddle," she needs to DM the organizer immediately:
    > *"Hey, I see there's a sync for [Project X] at 2 PM. I want to make sure I don't miss any context on the new feature—mind if I join?"*
* **The "Notes" Tactic:** If she misses a spontaneous hallway conversation, she must publicly ask in the project channel:
    > *"I saw you guys discussing the database migration. Was a decision made? Can someone summarize it here so I can align my code?"*
    * (This signals: "I am paying attention, and I expect to be informed.")

### 4. The "Design Doc" Infiltration
The best time to insert yourself into a project is during the **Design Document** phase (before any code is written).

* **What she should do:**
    1.  Read the Design Doc / RFC (Request for Comments) immediately when it drops.
    2.  Leave **3-4 comments/questions** on the doc within 24 hours.
    3.  This forces the authors to reply to her and recognize her as an active participant. It "validates" her ticket to the kickoff meeting.

### 5. Casual Script to "Lock In" Her Spot
She should say this to the Tech Lead of the upcoming project *now*, before it starts:

> "Hey [Lead Name], I know [Project Y] is coming up next quarter. I’m really interested in the [Specific Technical Area, e.g., caching layer] of that. I’d love to take ownership of that piece. Can you loop me in when the early architecture discussions start so I can prep for it?"

### Summary Checklist for Her:
1.  **Pick a lane:** Don't just "join the project." Own a specific component (API, UI, DB, Testing).
2.  **Kill the distractions:** Tell the manager she cannot do "floater" work during this project.
3.  **Invite herself:** Do not wait for a calendar invite. Ask for it.
